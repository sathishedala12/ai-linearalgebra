CHAPTER 01

Why visualize

Summary statistics lie. Pictures don't. The most famous lesson in data visualization is also the most important one.

In 1973, statistician Francis Anscombe published four small datasets that became immortal. Each has the same number of points, the same mean for both variables, the same variance, the same correlation, and the same regression line. By every numerical summary they are identical. Plot them and you immediately see they're radically different: one is a clean line, one is a perfect curve, one is a line dragged off course by a single outlier, and one is a vertical pillar that shouldn't have a regression line at all. The numbers agreed. The pictures disagreed. Pictures were right.

Figure 1.1Anscombe's quartet. All four datasets share mean, variance, correlation, and regression line — yet they're obviously different the moment you plot them.

1.1Two kinds of plotting

  • Exploration. Plots you make to understand. Fast, ugly, many, throwaway.
  • Presentation. Plots you make to communicate. Slow, polished, few, durable.

Most of this book is exploration — that's where the reps happen. Chapters 11 and 12 cover the transition to presentation quality.

1.2The Python plotting stack

Application

Every dataset hides surprises in its tails, its outliers, its skew. Numerical summaries average those surprises away. Make plotting your first move on any new data — before you compute, before you model, before you write a single conclusion.

CHAPTER 02

Matplotlib — the engine

Two APIs, only one of which you should use. Pick the right one and matplotlib stops feeling chaotic.

2.1Setup

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np
# In Jupyter, this is automatic. In scripts, end with plt.show()

2.2Figure and axes — the mental model

A figure is the canvas. An axes is a single plot living inside it ("axes" is singular here — a quirk of matplotlib's vocabulary). A figure can contain one or many axes. Every plot happens on some axes.

2.3The two APIs

pyplot API — implicit, MATLAB-style

plt.plot([1, 2, 3], [1, 4, 9])
plt.xlabel("x"); plt.ylabel("y"); plt.title("squared")
plt.show()

Fine for quick interactive use. Becomes awkward fast: which figure is "current"? Where do customizations apply?

Object-oriented API — explicit

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5))
ax.plot([1, 2, 3], [1, 4, 9])
ax.set_xlabel("x"); ax.set_ylabel("y"); ax.set_title("squared")

Use this one. Always. Every action targets a specific ax, customizations are unambiguous, and growing from one plot to many takes one extra argument.

the rule

Always start with fig, ax = plt.subplots(). Two more characters than plt.plot — and it saves you from rewriting your code the moment you want a second subplot or a custom size.

2.4Multiple subplots

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 3, figsize=(12, 6))

axes[0, 0].plot(x, y1)
axes[0, 1].scatter(x, y2)
axes[1, 2].hist(y3)

# Loop pattern
for ax, col in zip(axes.flat, df.columns):
    ax.hist(df[col]); ax.set_title(col)

fig.tight_layout()

2.5The anatomy of an axes

ax.set_xlim(0, 10); ax.set_ylim(0, 100)
ax.set_xlabel("time (s)"); ax.set_ylabel("value")
ax.set_title("my plot")
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax.legend()
ax.spines["top"].set_visible(False)
ax.spines["right"].set_visible(False)

2.6Saving

fig.savefig("plot.png", dpi=150, bbox_inches="tight")
fig.savefig("plot.pdf")        # vector — perfect for print
fig.savefig("plot.svg")        # vector — perfect for web
Application

Once you commit to the OO API, every matplotlib weirdness in Stack Overflow ("why doesn't my title appear?", "why is my second plot blank?") disappears. The few extra characters per line pay back in clarity by the third plot of any session.

CHAPTER 03

The plot types you'll use

Five plot types cover ninety percent of all visualization. Master them and the rest is variation.

3.1Line plot — change over a continuous axis

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(dates, revenue, label="revenue", color="#28c595", linewidth=2)
ax.plot(dates, costs,   label="costs",   color="#ec7548", linestyle="--")
ax.legend()
ax.set_ylabel("USD millions")

Best for time series, ordered sequences, anything where the x-axis has natural continuity.

3.2Scatter plot — two-variable relationships

ax.scatter(df["age"], df["income"], alpha=0.4, s=20)

Set alpha below 1 whenever you have more than a few hundred points — without transparency, dense clusters look identical to sparse ones.

3.3Bar chart — comparing categories

ax.bar(categories, values, color="#28c595")
ax.barh(categories, values)        # horizontal — better with many or long labels

Always sort bars by value unless the categories have a natural order (months, weekdays, ratings). Unordered bars waste the reader's attention.

3.4Histogram — distribution of one variable

ax.hist(df["price"], bins=30, color="#9d96e6", edgecolor="white")

The bin count matters. Too few and you miss the shape; too many and the histogram becomes noisy. Default of 10 is usually wrong — try 30 first.

3.5Box plot — distribution summary across groups

ax.boxplot([df[df["region"] == r]["sales"] for r in regions], labels=regions)

Compresses a lot of information — median, quartiles, range, outliers — into a small footprint. Seaborn makes this much easier (Chapter 6).

3.6The chart-type cheatsheet

QuestionRight plot
What's the distribution of X?histogram, KDE, ECDF
Does X change over time?line plot
Is X related to Y?scatter (+ regression line)
How does X differ by category?bar (means), box (full distribution)
How does X relate to Y across categories?scatter with hue, FacetGrid
What's the correlation structure?heatmap, pairplot
Application

Three rules that eliminate most bad visualizations you'd otherwise make: never use pie charts, never use 3-D bars, never use dual-axis line plots with two different scales. Apply these consistently and your charts immediately get more honest.

CHAPTER 04

Customizing

Style, color, annotation. Knowing where the knobs are is the difference between a default-looking plot and something a colleague will trust.

4.1Styles — instant good defaults

plt.style.use("seaborn-v0_8-whitegrid")   # clean, modern
plt.style.use("ggplot")                   # R-flavored
plt.style.use("dark_background")          # for dark slides
plt.style.available                           # list all built-in styles

Importing seaborn at the top of a notebook also changes matplotlib's defaults to something noticeably better. Worth doing every session.

4.2Colors — palettes and rules

ax.plot(x, y, color="#28c595")
ax.plot(x, y, color="tab:blue")
ax.scatter(x, y, c=values, cmap="viridis")        # sequential
ax.scatter(x, y, c=values, cmap="RdBu_r")         # diverging

Three rules:

  • Don't use rainbow. "jet" looks pretty but distorts perception. Use viridis or plasma.
  • Sequential for ordered data, diverging for data centered on zero. Never the reverse.
  • Encode the same variable consistently. If East is teal in one plot, East stays teal everywhere.

4.3Labels, titles, fonts

ax.set_xlabel("date", fontsize=12)
ax.set_ylabel("revenue (M USD)", fontsize=12)
ax.set_title("Quarterly revenue", fontsize=14, fontweight="bold")

# Global defaults
plt.rcParams["font.size"] = 12
plt.rcParams["figure.dpi"] = 100
plt.rcParams["axes.spines.top"] = False
plt.rcParams["axes.spines.right"] = False

4.4Annotations — point things out

ax.annotate(
    "all-time high",
    xy=(date_peak, value_peak),
    xytext=(date_peak, value_peak + 10),
    arrowprops={"arrowstyle": "->", "color": "#6b7385"},
    fontsize=11,
)
ax.axhline(target, color="#ec7548", linestyle="--", label="target")
ax.axvline(launch_date, color="#9d96e6", linestyle="--")

One labeled annotation often communicates more than a paragraph of caption. Add one to every chart that's going somewhere outside your notebook.

4.5Layout

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(10, 8),
                          sharex=True, sharey=True)
fig.suptitle("By region", fontsize=14)
fig.tight_layout()
defaults to set once

Put this at the top of your notebooks: sns.set_theme(style="whitegrid") for clean defaults; plt.rcParams["figure.figsize"] = (8, 5) for a sensible default size; and the spine hides above. Set them once — every chart benefits.

CHAPTER 05

Seaborn — statistical visuals

A layer on top of matplotlib that knows about DataFrames. Where matplotlib makes you build a plot piece by piece, seaborn lets you describe what you want and figures out the rest.

5.1Setup

import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
sns.set_theme(style="whitegrid", palette="deep")

5.2The big idea — data + variable names

sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="age", y="income", hue="region")

Three things make seaborn dramatically more productive than matplotlib for EDA:

  1. It accepts a DataFrame and column names. No df["x"].values, no manual filtering.
  2. The hue, size, style arguments automatically encode another column. Categorical coloring for free.
  3. It computes statistics for you. Confidence intervals, regression lines, density estimates — built in.

5.3The function families

FamilyFunctionsUse for
relationalscatterplot, lineplot, relplottwo-variable relationships
distributionhistplot, kdeplot, ecdfplot, displotshape of one variable
categoricalbarplot, boxplot, violinplot, stripplot, swarmplot, catplotvariable across groups
regressionregplot, lmplot, residplotfits and residuals
matrixheatmap, clustermap2-D summaries
multi-panelFacetGrid, pairplot, jointplot, PairGridgrids of related plots

5.4Axes-level vs figure-level

# Axes-level — your default, composes with matplotlib
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="x", y="y", ax=ax)
ax.set_title("my plot")

# Figure-level — creates its own figure, multi-panel ready
sns.relplot(data=df, x="x", y="y", col="region", kind="scatter")
default to axes-level

Axes-level functions compose with regular matplotlib code. Only reach for figure-level functions when you specifically want their multi-panel magic.

Application

Seaborn was designed by someone who'd done a lot of EDA and gotten tired of typing the same matplotlib boilerplate. Every shortcut it provides is a paper cut someone fixed. Use it for exploration; you'll move 3-5× faster than equivalent matplotlib.

CHAPTER 06

Distributions

The shape of a single variable. Always start here — you can't reason about a column you haven't seen the distribution of.

6.1histplot — the workhorse

sns.histplot(data=df, x="price", bins=30)

# Compare distributions by category
sns.histplot(data=df, x="price", hue="region", multiple="stack")
sns.histplot(data=df, x="price", hue="region", multiple="dodge")
sns.histplot(data=df, x="price", hue="region", element="step", stat="density")

The multiple argument controls how categories show: "stack", "dodge", "layer", or "fill". Each tells a different story; pick the one matching your question.

6.2kdeplot — smoothed density

sns.kdeplot(data=df, x="price", hue="region", fill=True, alpha=0.3)

Better than a histogram for comparing distributions across groups; worse for seeing exact counts.

6.3ecdfplot — empirical CDF

sns.ecdfplot(data=df, x="price", hue="region")

Underused but excellent. An ECDF shows "what fraction of values is ≤ x" — no bins, no smoothing, no choice to make. The most honest way to plot a distribution.

6.4boxplot & violinplot — distribution summaries

sns.boxplot(data=df, x="region", y="price")
sns.violinplot(data=df, x="region", y="price", inner="quartile")

Box plots show quartiles and outliers. Violins show full density. Use violins when shape matters (bimodal? skewed?) and boxes when quartiles suffice.

6.5The distribution checklist for a new variable

  1. histplot — is the distribution unimodal? skewed? are there gaps?
  2. describe() — what are min, max, quartiles? do they make sense?
  3. ecdfplot or boxplot — where are the outliers?
  4. If grouped: kde or violin to compare across categories
Application

Three minutes per numeric column at the start of any analysis. Look at each distribution. Note what surprises you — the skews, outliers, gaps, multi-modality. These notes become the questions that drive everything that follows.

CHAPTER 07

Relationships

How two or three or four variables relate. The middle of any analysis where the real findings live.

7.1scatterplot — the foundation

sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="age", y="income")
sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="age", y="income", hue="region")
sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="age", y="income",
                hue="region", size="tenure", sizes=(20, 200))

Diminishing returns: four-variable scatterplots are still readable; five gets crowded; six becomes art. Don't push past four without a reason.

7.2lineplot — over an ordered variable

sns.lineplot(data=df, x="date", y="revenue", hue="region")

# Automatic confidence intervals from repeated measurements
sns.lineplot(data=df, x="day", y="value", hue="experiment", errorbar="ci")

Seaborn's lineplot automatically aggregates and adds confidence bands when you have repeated x-values. This one feature replaces a lot of manual groupby-then-plot code.

7.3regplot & lmplot — regression

sns.regplot(data=df, x="age", y="income")
# scatter + linear regression line + 95% CI band

sns.regplot(data=df, x="age", y="income", order=2)
sns.regplot(data=df, x="age", y="income", lowess=True)

# lmplot = regplot but figure-level — splits by category
sns.lmplot(data=df, x="age", y="income", hue="region", col="plan")

7.4heatmap — correlation structure

corr = df.corr(numeric_only=True)

sns.heatmap(corr, annot=True, fmt=".2f", cmap="RdBu_r",
            center=0, vmin=-1, vmax=1, square=True)

The classic correlation heatmap. Always center diverging palettes on zero with center=0 — otherwise positive and negative correlations get the same visual weight and the picture lies.

Application

The first plot of any unfamiliar dataset should be a correlation heatmap. It tells you which variables move together — and that map of relationships guides every scatter plot you make next.

CHAPTER 08

Categorical comparisons

How a numeric variable differs across categories. The bread-and-butter of business analytics.

8.1barplot — means with error bars

sns.barplot(data=df, x="region", y="sales", errorbar="ci")
sns.barplot(data=df, x="region", y="sales", hue="product")

Default: seaborn computes means. Pass estimator=np.median or any callable for a different summary. Pass errorbar=None to hide bars when they're not the point.

8.2countplot — frequency of categories

sns.countplot(data=df, y="region", order=df["region"].value_counts().index)
# horizontal + sorted = readable

8.3boxplot & violinplot per group

sns.boxplot(data=df, x="region", y="sales", hue="product")
sns.violinplot(data=df, x="region", y="sales", hue="product", split=True)

split=True on violinplot is a small miracle when comparing exactly two categories per group — half-violins per side, very readable.

8.4stripplot & swarmplot — every point

sns.stripplot(data=df, x="region", y="sales", alpha=0.3)
sns.swarmplot(data=df, x="region", y="sales")

# Common pattern — box plus all points
sns.boxplot(data=df, x="region", y="sales")
sns.stripplot(data=df, x="region", y="sales", color="black", alpha=0.3)

8.5pointplot — group means connected

sns.pointplot(data=df, x="quarter", y="sales", hue="region")

8.6catplot — the figure-level umbrella

sns.catplot(data=df, x="region", y="sales",
            kind="box",        # or "bar", "violin", "strip", "swarm", "point"
            col="product")

One function that wraps all the categorical plots with multi-panel faceting via col and row.

order matters

Categorical bars default to data order. Pass order=["small", "medium", "large"] for ordinal data, or order=df.groupby("x")["y"].mean().sort_values().index for ranked bars. Alphabetical order is almost never what you want.

CHAPTER 09

Multi-panel plots

Grids of related plots. The most powerful EDA tools in seaborn — and the ones that pay back fastest.

9.1pairplot — every variable against every other

sns.pairplot(df, hue="region")
sns.pairplot(df, vars=["age", "income", "tenure"], hue="churn",
             diag_kind="kde", plot_kws={"alpha": 0.5})

Diagonal: distributions. Off-diagonal: scatter plots of all pairs. One line gives you an overview of an entire numeric DataFrame. Slow on wide datasets — restrict with vars=.

9.2jointplot — one pair, with marginals

sns.jointplot(data=df, x="age", y="income", kind="scatter")
sns.jointplot(data=df, x="age", y="income", kind="hex")        # for many points
sns.jointplot(data=df, x="age", y="income", kind="reg")

The center shows the joint distribution; the margins show each variable's own. Best for examining a single important pair.

9.3FacetGrid — manual paneling

g = sns.FacetGrid(df, col="region", row="product",
                  margin_titles=True, height=3)
g.map_dataframe(sns.scatterplot, x="age", y="income")
g.add_legend()

9.4The figure-level functions revisited

sns.relplot(data=df, x="age", y="income", hue="plan", col="region", kind="scatter")
sns.displot(data=df, x="price", hue="region", col="product", kind="hist")
sns.catplot(data=df, x="region", y="sales", col="product", kind="box")

These three handle 90% of multi-panel needs. Pass col= to split into columns, row= for rows, col_wrap=N to wrap a long list into a grid.

Application

Faceting reveals interactions that single plots hide. "Does age affect income equally in every region?" becomes obvious from a scatterplot faceted by region. The same question is invisible from one global scatter. This is why col= exists.

CHAPTER 10

The EDA playbook

A repeatable sequence for the first hour with any new dataset. Less guesswork, more discovery.

10.1Step 1 — shape, dtypes, missingness

df.shape
df.dtypes
df.head()
df.isna().sum().sort_values(ascending=False)
df.describe(include="all")

Five lines that prevent half the bugs you'd otherwise hit. Don't skip them even when you "know" the dataset.

10.2Step 2 — distribution of every numeric column

num_cols = df.select_dtypes(include="number").columns
fig, axes = plt.subplots(len(num_cols) // 3 + 1, 3, figsize=(14, 8))
for ax, col in zip(axes.flat, num_cols):
    sns.histplot(df[col], ax=ax, bins=30)
    ax.set_title(col)
fig.tight_layout()

One grid showing every numeric distribution. Look for skews, outliers, gaps, suspicious zeros, multi-modality. Write down what surprises you.

10.3Step 3 — frequency of every categorical

cat_cols = df.select_dtypes(include=["object", "category"]).columns
for col in cat_cols:
    print(col, ":", df[col].nunique(), "unique")
    print(df[col].value_counts().head(10))

For each categorical, the top values and the count of uniques. Spot dirty values (capitalization variants, typos, encoding artifacts), check cardinality (is "user_id" actually categorical or just high-cardinality?).

10.4Step 4 — correlation heatmap

sns.heatmap(df.corr(numeric_only=True), annot=True, fmt=".2f",
            cmap="RdBu_r", center=0, square=True)

The map for the next stage. Strong correlations → make scatter plots. Surprising correlations → investigate why.

10.5Step 5 — pairplot of suspects

sns.pairplot(df[["target", "feature_a", "feature_b", "feature_c"]],
             hue="target", diag_kind="kde")

Pick the 5-8 most interesting columns from steps 2-4 and pairplot them. Patterns jump out.

10.6Step 6 — target vs each predictor

# For a numeric target
for col in num_cols:
    sns.regplot(data=df, x=col, y="target"); plt.show()

# For a categorical target
for col in num_cols:
    sns.kdeplot(data=df, x=col, hue="target", fill=True); plt.show()

This is where features that matter for prediction reveal themselves.

10.7Step 7 — write the findings

Open a markdown cell. Write three to five sentences for each surprise you've found. "Price is right-skewed, with a long tail of luxury items above $1000." "Region affects price strongly — West is 40% higher than East." "Tenure correlates 0.62 with revenue but only in the Premium plan."

This list is the deliverable of the EDA phase. Everything downstream — modeling, business decisions, dashboards — flows from it.

Application

The playbook works on any tabular dataset, in any domain. Run it for an hour on a new dataset and you'll know more about it than most people who've worked with it for months. The discipline of writing findings — not just looking at charts — is what turns plotting into insight.

CHAPTER 11

Presentation-ready plots

The same plot that worked for your eyes during EDA needs work before it lands in a slide deck. Five upgrades make the difference.

11.1Upgrade 1 — a real title and labels

EDA: ax.set_title("price"). Presentation: ax.set_title("Median price is 22% higher in Q4 than any other quarter"). The title carries the conclusion, not the variable name. The reader knows what the plot is at a glance.

11.2Upgrade 2 — remove chart junk

ax.spines["top"].set_visible(False)
ax.spines["right"].set_visible(False)
ax.grid(axis="y", alpha=0.3)        # grid on one axis only
ax.tick_params(length=0)             # no tick marks

Everything that doesn't carry information is noise. Tufte's data-ink ratio: maximize the ink that shows your numbers, minimize everything else.

11.3Upgrade 3 — direct labels instead of legends

for region, group in df.groupby("region"):
    ax.plot(group["date"], group["value"], label=region)
    # Place the label at the end of the line
    ax.annotate(region, xy=(group["date"].iloc[-1], group["value"].iloc[-1]),
                xytext=(5, 0), textcoords="offset points", fontsize=10)

For line plots, putting the label right next to the line is far easier to read than a legend in the corner. The reader's eye doesn't have to bounce.

11.4Upgrade 4 — color with purpose

In EDA, color encodes a category. In presentation, color directs attention. Make the line you're talking about bold and saturated, and gray out the rest:

for region, group in df.groupby("region"):
    color = "#28c595" if region == "West" else "#cccccc"
    lw = 2.5 if region == "West" else 1
    ax.plot(group["date"], group["value"], color=color, linewidth=lw)

11.5Upgrade 5 — annotate the takeaway

ax.annotate(
    "+40% YoY",
    xy=(growth_point_x, growth_point_y),
    xytext=(10, 20), textcoords="offset points",
    fontsize=12, fontweight="bold",
    arrowprops={"arrowstyle": "->", "color": "#6b7385"},
)

The chart should narrate itself. If the reader has to compute "this is 40% higher than that," you've outsourced your job. Do the math, write the conclusion on the chart.

11.6Save for the medium

fig.savefig("chart.png", dpi=200, bbox_inches="tight")        # slides
fig.savefig("chart.pdf", bbox_inches="tight")                # print
fig.savefig("chart.svg", bbox_inches="tight")                # web
Application

Promote a chart from EDA to presentation when you decide it's worth a stranger's ten seconds. Most charts you make will never be promoted; the few that matter deserve all five upgrades. The investment ratio is roughly 10:1 — that's the price of communication.

CHAPTER 12

Mistakes & how to avoid them

A short field guide to plots that look fine but mislead the reader. Knowing these makes you a better consumer of other people's charts too.

12.1Mistake 1 — truncated y-axes

A bar chart of "support ratings: 4.7 vs 4.8" with the y-axis starting at 4.5 makes a 2% gap look like a chasm. Bars should start at zero. Lines and scatter can start anywhere — but always pause and ask "would my axis range deceive someone?"

12.2Mistake 2 — overplotting in dense scatter

10,000 scatter points stacked on top of each other look like 1,000. Solutions: alpha=0.2, hex bins (jointplot kind="hex"), 2-D KDE, or subsampling.

12.3Mistake 3 — dual-axis line charts

"Revenue on the left axis, customer count on the right" — the two scales create false correlations. Either two stacked plots sharing an x-axis, or normalize both to the same scale (percent change from a baseline) on a single axis.

12.4Mistake 4 — rainbow palettes for ordered data

The "jet" colormap is non-monotonic in perceived lightness — values that look equally bright actually represent different numbers. Use viridis, plasma, cividis for sequential data, RdBu_r centered on zero for diverging.

12.5Mistake 5 — pie charts beyond three slices

Human eyes can't reliably compare angles. With three slices it's fine. With seven it's a guessing game. Use a sorted bar chart instead — every time.

12.6Mistake 6 — categorical bars in alphabetical order

The default sort. Almost never useful. Sort by value (for "which is highest?"), or by an ordinal scale (small/medium/large), or by time (Jan/Feb/Mar). Alphabetical sort is the absence of a decision.

12.7Mistake 7 — missing units

"Revenue" or "Revenue (M USD)"? "Time" or "Time (seconds)"? Always include units in axis labels. The reader shouldn't have to guess whether your y-axis is in thousands or millions.

12.8Mistake 8 — too many lines on one plot

Above five lines, a line chart becomes a tangled mess. Solutions: facet (small multiples), highlight one line and gray the rest, or use a heatmap of (group × time).

12.9Mistake 9 — not handling missing data visibly

If 30% of values are NaN, your histogram silently drops them and your reader can't tell. Plot a separate "% missing" bar or note the count in the title. Hiding missingness hides the most important fact about your data.

12.10What to do next

You now have a complete plotting toolkit. To go from "knows" to "fluent":

  1. Plot first. Make plotting your first move on any new dataset.
  2. Save your one-liners. Build a personal cheatsheet of the seaborn calls you use most.
  3. Read more plots. Critique published charts — what works, what misleads, what could be clearer.
