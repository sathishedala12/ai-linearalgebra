CHAPTER 01

The project

Before opening the data, set the frame: what's the question, what's the dataset, what does success look like? Five minutes of planning saves an hour of wandering.

The Kaggle "House Prices — Advanced Regression Techniques" competition uses the Ames Iowa Housing dataset, compiled by Dean De Cock. It contains 1,460 training rows and 79 features describing residential homes sold in Ames, Iowa between 2006 and 2010. The target is SalePrice — the final sale price in dollars. The competition asks for the lowest root-mean-squared-error on the log of the price. We're not going to compete; we're going to do the part that comes before any model: understand what's actually in this data.

1.1The question

The framing question for this EDA: "If we wanted to predict the sale price of a house in this dataset, which features would matter most, and what would we have to do to use them?" Every chart and every summary that follows is in service of that question.

1.2The dataset at a glance

1.4What success looks like

EDA is "done" when:

  • You can name the top 5-10 features most related to the target.
  • You know where the missing data is and whether it's missing-at-random.
  • You've identified outliers and decided what to do about them.
  • You've decided whether the target needs transformation.
  • You have a written list of feature-engineering ideas to try.

That list is the deliverable. Everything else is process.

Application

Treat every new dataset like a new city. The EDA is the walk-around — you're learning the neighborhoods, noticing where things are clustered, finding the surprises. The model comes later. People who skip the walk-around build worse models, not faster ones.

CHAPTER 04

The target — SalePrice

Before exploring features, understand the thing you're trying to predict. Skew, range, distribution shape — these decisions cascade into everything that follows.

4.1The five-number summary

df["SalePrice"].describe().round()
count      1460
mean     180921
std       79443
min       34900
25%      129975
50%      163000
75%      214000
max      755000

Mean (180k) is well above median (163k) — classic right-skew. The max is more than 20× the min. The 75th percentile is 214k but the max is 755k — meaning there's a long tail of expensive houses.

4.2The distribution

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 4))
sns.histplot(df["SalePrice"], bins=40, ax=axes[0])
axes[0].set_title(f"SalePrice — skew = {df['SalePrice'].skew():.2f}")

sns.histplot(np.log1p(df["SalePrice"]), bins=40, ax=axes[1])
axes[1].set_title(f"log(SalePrice) — skew = {np.log1p(df['SalePrice']).skew():.2f}")
fig.tight_layout()

Skewness measures asymmetry: 0 is symmetric, positive means a right tail. The raw SalePrice has skew ≈ 1.88 — heavily right-skewed. After log transformation, skew drops to about 0.12 — essentially symmetric.

finding 4

SalePrice is right-skewed (skew ≈ 1.88). log1p(SalePrice) is approximately normal (skew ≈ 0.12). The Kaggle metric is RMSE on log(SalePrice) — confirming that modeling on the log scale is the right approach. Predictions will be made in log space and exponentiated back.

4.3Why log matters

Modeling SalePrice directly with a linear model would weight a $50k error on a $200k house the same as a $50k error on a $700k house. Modeling log(SalePrice) instead means a 10% error matters equally everywhere — which matches both intuition and the competition metric.

4.4The outliers in the target

df.nlargest(10, "SalePrice")[["SalePrice", "GrLivArea", "OverallQual", "Neighborhood"]]

The most expensive houses are all in NoRidge and NridgHt with above-grade living area over 4000 sq ft and overall quality 10. These aren't data errors — they're real luxury properties. Keep them, but watch how the model handles them.

4.5Save the target choice

y = np.log1p(df["SalePrice"])
df_features = df.drop(columns=["SalePrice", "Id"])

From here on, when we talk about "the target," we mean log1p(SalePrice).

CHAPTER 08

Outliers & skew

Two separate problems often discussed as one. Outliers are wrong or special rows. Skew is a property of a distribution. Different fixes for each.

8.1The outlier checklist

Three categories. Treat each differently:

  • Data errors — a price of $1, a year of 9999, a square footage of -50. Fix or drop. Always.
  • Structural anomalies — the Partial sales above. Drop if they're not the population you care about.
  • Genuine extremes — the $755k luxury house. Keep. Use models that handle them well (regularized linear or gradient boosting).

8.2Checking each top feature

top_features = ["GrLivArea", "LotArea", "TotalBsmtSF", "1stFlrSF", "GarageArea"]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, len(top_features), figsize=(15, 4))
for ax, col in zip(axes, top_features):
    sns.boxplot(y=df[col], ax=ax)
    ax.set_title(col)

LotArea has a few extreme outliers (the five-acre lot). They're real properties, not errors. Note them, but don't drop them. A log transformation on LotArea is more useful than removing them.

Many features are heavily right-skewed. For linear models, log-transforming them often helps. For tree-based models, it doesn't matter — trees are invariant to monotonic transformations of features.

8.4The fix — log1p for positive skewed features

skewed = skewness[skewness.abs() > 0.75].index.tolist()

for col in skewed:
    if (df_features[col] >= 0).all():
        df_features[col] = np.log1p(df_features[col])

log1p(x) = log(1 + x) handles zero values cleanly. Use it whenever you might log-transform something that can be zero.

finding 11

About 25 numeric features are notably skewed. For a linear model: log-transform all positive ones with skew > 0.75. For tree-based models: don't bother. The Partial-sale outliers (rows 523, 1298) should be dropped regardless.

CHAPTER 09

Feature engineering ideas

EDA hands the modeler a list. Each idea below was discovered through the previous chapters — not invented from thin air.

9.1Combine related areas

df_features["TotalSF"] = (
    df_features["TotalBsmtSF"] + df_features["1stFlrSF"] + df_features["2ndFlrSF"]
)
df_features["TotalBath"] = (
    df_features["FullBath"] + 0.5 * df_features["HalfBath"]
    + df_features["BsmtFullBath"] + 0.5 * df_features["BsmtHalfBath"]
)
df_features["TotalPorchSF"] = (
    df_features["OpenPorchSF"] + df_features["EnclosedPorch"]
    + df_features["3SsnPorch"] + df_features["ScreenPorch"] + df_features["WoodDeckSF"]
)

Where multiple columns measure the same conceptual thing, sum them. TotalSF typically becomes the single strongest predictor in the model.

9.2Age and remodel age

df_features["HouseAge"]    = df["YrSold"] - df_features["YearBuilt"]
df_features["RemodelAge"]  = df["YrSold"] - df_features["YearRemodAdd"]
df_features["IsRemodeled"] = (df_features["YearBuilt"] != df_features["YearRemodAdd"]).astype(int)

9.3Presence flags from mostly-zero features

df_features["HasPool"]       = (df_features["PoolArea"] > 0).astype(int)
df_features["HasGarage"]     = (df_features["GarageArea"] > 0).astype(int)
df_features["HasBasement"]   = (df_features["TotalBsmtSF"] > 0).astype(int)
df_features["HasFireplace"]  = (df_features["Fireplaces"] > 0).astype(int)
df_features["Has2ndFloor"]   = (df_features["2ndFlrSF"] > 0).astype(int)

The mostly-zero columns from chapter 5 carry two pieces of information: whether the feature exists at all, and (if it does) how big it is. Splitting them into a flag plus a size lets models use both.

9.4Quality interactions

df_features["QualBySF"]    = df_features["OverallQual"] * df_features["TotalSF"]
df_features["QualByAge"]   = df_features["OverallQual"] * df_features["HouseAge"]

A high-quality 4000-sq-ft house is worth more per square foot than a low-quality one of the same size. Interaction features capture that.

9.5Neighborhood tier

neighborhood_median = df.groupby("Neighborhood")["SalePrice"].median()
tiers = pd.qcut(neighborhood_median, 3, labels=["low", "mid", "high"])
df_features["NeighborhoodTier"] = df_features["Neighborhood"].map(tiers)

For models that struggle with 25-level categoricals, collapsing to a 3-level "tier" preserves most of the signal at a fraction of the feature cost.

9.6The full engineering list

Every idea above came directly from a finding in chapters 3-8. The discipline is to be able to point to why each engineered feature exists. If you can't, drop it — it's adding noise.

### Target
- Right-skewed (skew 1.88); log1p transform brings to skew 0.12
- Model on log1p(SalePrice), exponentiate predictions back
- Range: $34,900 to $755,000

### Missingness
- Mostly structural — NA means "feature absent" per the data dictionary
- PoolQC, MiscFeature, Alley, Fence, FireplaceQu, Garage*, Bsmt* → fill with "None"/0
- LotFrontage (17.7% missing) → impute by neighborhood median
- Electrical (one missing) → impute with mode

### Top predictors (correlation with log price)
1. OverallQual (0.79) — single best feature
2. GrLivArea (0.71) — strong linear after Partial-sale outlier removal
3. TotalSF [engineered] (~0.83 expected) — sum of basement + floors
4. ExterQual, KitchenQual, BsmtQual (0.62–0.68) — ordinal-encode these
5. GarageCars / GarageArea (0.64 / 0.62) — highly correlated; pick one
6. YearBuilt (0.52) — slightly nonlinear; use HouseAge = YrSold - YearBuilt

### Categorical insight
- Neighborhood: 25 levels, 3.5× spread in median price
- Quality columns (Ex/Gd/TA/Fa/Po) must be ordinal, not one-hot
- SaleCondition matters: Partial sales are structurally different

### Outliers
- Drop rows 523 and 1298 — Partial sale, GrLivArea > 4000, low price
- Keep all genuine luxury outliers — they're not errors

### Feature engineering ideas
- TotalSF = TotalBsmtSF + 1stFlrSF + 2ndFlrSF
- TotalBath = FullBath + 0.5*HalfBath + BsmtFullBath + 0.5*BsmtHalfBath
- HouseAge, RemodelAge, IsRemodeled
- HasPool / HasGarage / HasBasement / HasFireplace flags
- QualBySF and QualByAge interactions
- NeighborhoodTier (low/mid/high from median price)
- log1p the ~25 skewed numeric features

### Modeling notes
- Two model families to try: regularized linear (Ridge/Lasso/ElasticNet) and gradient boosting
- Linear models need the log transforms and ordinal encodings
- GBM doesn't care about transforms but benefits from interaction features
- Cross-validate carefully: only 1460 rows total
- Evaluation: RMSE on log(SalePrice), per the Kaggle metric

11.2What still lives in EDA territory

Even during modeling, you'll return to EDA techniques constantly:

  • Residual plots — after fitting a model, plot residuals vs each feature. Find structure the model missed.
  • Feature importance bar charts — sanity-check that the model's top features match your EDA's top predictors.
  • Partial dependence plots — how does the predicted price change as one feature varies?
  • Error analysis — which rows did the model get most wrong, and why?

EDA isn't a phase you finish — it's a mode you return to whenever the model surprises you.
